# Character Creation

## Converting Old-School D&D to Modern Days

*_Ascending AC:__ Your ascending AC is equal to 19 minus your descending AC. For example leather armor class of 7 equals AC 12 for modern d20 + To Hit vs. AC attack rolls.

__To Hit Bonus:__ Your To Hit bonus is equal to 19 minus your THAC0. For example level 1 Fighter has a THAC0 of 19, and their To Hit bonus equals +0 for modern d20 + To Hit vs. AC attack rolls.

## Rolling Scores

Scores are rolled 3d6 in order, no exceptions. You can adjust scores afterwards; see below.

Each player rolls three separate sets of scores and writes them down; the DM will collect unused sets and share them among all players. Any player may opt to take an unused set of scores from another player if their own set of scores does not match their expectation. When a character dies and a new one has to be created, three set of scores are rolled as well but the two unused are discarded.

## Adjust Scores

Reduce an ability score by 2 to gain 1 adjustment point; you may reduce each score once. Spend 1 adjustment point to increase an ability score by +1.

## Starting Health

All human and common demi-human characters of civilized settlements and civilizations in the world have an extra 1d4 hit die, called racial hit die and rolled upon character creation. Uncivilized races that live in harsh conditions or have oppressive societies (for example, Orcs and Drow) start with a d6 racial hit die. Other races may have racial hit die based on size (tiny 1, small 1d2, large 1d8, huge 2d6), or age (human child 1, human teen 1d2). For monsters, this racial health is part of their Hit Dice as indicated in their stat block. Characters, typically commoners without a class, only rely on these racial hit points. The racial hit points have a minimum of 1 hit point. The Constitution modifier does not apply to the racial hit die.

All characters that start at 1st level or higher in a class take maximum hit points from their class hit die at 1st level. At higher levels, class Hit Dice are rolled as normal.

When you roll a hit die to increase hit points at gaining a higher level, you may reroll the result if it rolls 1 or 2. You may reroll only once, and you take the new result.

## Starting Spells

When creating a new character with Arcane or Divine Spellcasting, the player must choose four player-favorite spells of the first level and roll d4 to randomly pick which spell they start with. The choice can be made only from the __Player’s Tome;__ unique and custom spells exist, but they must be found or researched during the game. If the player does not like the rolled result, they can take Sleep (as an arcane spellcaster) or Cure Light Wounds (as a divine spellcaster).

If they know more than one beginning spell (__Advanced Spell Book Rules__, pg. 124), they must roll randomly for those bonus spells on the complete list for any spell level they can cast, using an appropriately sized die (d6, d8, or d12). Duplicate rolls are rerolled until each beginner spell is unique.

__When a Magic User or Cleric attains a new level that adds new slots or unlocks access to a new spell level, they do not get a free spell like in modern-sensibilities games.__ They must find a teacher or site of worship and access to scrolls, spell books, or holy texts to gain a new spell via copying. Alternatively, they may research the spell, which typically costs 1000 gp per spell level and takes two weeks of training, practice, or devout worship.

> Unlike later editions of D&D, Clerics and Druids do not receive access to every spell available to their level, and must seek out and develop spells though similar methods like the Magic User, like discovering scrolls or caches of knowledge (holy texts or dolmens of wisdom, in their case). To partially remedy this, in-world monasteries and druid circles often serve as places where Clerics and Druids can share all collected knowledge within their religion, cult, or circle; unlike Magic Users and Illusionists who are typically selfish and paranoid of each other. Unlike spending money to research and learn new spells in such way, Clerics and Druids may choose to engage in Philanthropy for the same value, or take on quests and service to further the goals of the local temple or druid circle.

## Alignment

The meaning of alignment has changed. Lawful and Chaotic represent powerful inclinations that align your character with the otherworldly forces of Law and Chaos, and actions that conflict with your alignment are considered anathema. Alignment anathema may still be transgressed under duress but requires character development and atonement for your acts, which is unnecessary for neutral characters. Extraordinary roleplay of your alignment may grant you divine interventions or a role in the grand conflict between powers of Law and Chaos. Alignment languages are no longer languages but the ability to sense the motive and context of a character’s actions of the exact alignment. You only must choose a Lawful alignment if you play a Paladin, and you are expected to act as Lawful (but may be Neutral in secret) if you are Cleric.

Most humans and demi-humans are neutral by birth, driven by their self-centered needs and personal goals. Even among adventurers, about 70% of all characters will be neutral; if you wish to leave it to chance, make a d20 roll and pick another alignment if you roll 15+. The frequency is as low as 95% among non-adventurers and ordinary men; roll for a natural 20 if you make an ordinary character. Lawful Clerics (and similar classes with alignment requirements, except for Paladins) can choose to be Neutral but are required by their stature to pretend to be Lawful. Such characters may never benefit from Divine Interventions and similar extraordinary circumstances.

## Encumbrance

Use flat coin weight (cn) with clear movement thresholds. Strength modifies the first three thresholds. The 1,600 cn threshold is an absolute maximum. Above 1,600 cn you are Overburdened and can only step or crawl (5') as long as you take no other action.

Base Thresholds

| Encumbrance (cn) | Movement   |
| ---------------- | ---------- |
| Up to 400        | 120' (40') |
| Up to 600        | 90' (30')  |
| Up to 800        | 60' (20')  |
| Up to 1,600      | 30' (10')  |

Strength Modifiers (applied to 400/600/800 only)

| Strength   | Threshold Modifier |
| ---------- | ------------------ |
| 18         | +50%               |
| 16-17      | +30%               |
| 13-15      | +15%               |
| 8-12       | +0%                |
| 5-7        | -25%               |
| 4 or lower | -40%               |

You must manage two (or three, during exploration) levels of equipment weight during play, and calculate movement rate for each cumulatively:

- Your armor, personal weapons and worn items (often magical, and typically of negligible weight) that you must carry at all times, except during rest when medium and heavy armor must be taken off to afford comfortable rest (you may sleep in armor should you prefer but you recover no hp from that rest). These items affect your movement rate during combat. Typically items up to 10cn per unit can be worn in pockets, belts and pouches, and be immediately available as part of the action, as long as you can spare one hand or more (up to the DM).
- Your backpack and all items that can be immediately dropped at the start of combat as a free action unless surprised, are assumed to be immediately dropped and left unattended during combat unless you decide otherwise. You typically would decide otherwise if having to flee risks leaving behind expensive equipment or treasure, or unexpected enemy could steal an item while you are engaged in combat. It takes a full round of action to take something out of your backpack, plus your two hands must be free. Remember that having this equipment on you at all times is a liability and may prevent you from fleeing if it modifies your movement rate to worse than your enemies, or below that of your allies.
- Any equipment that is not carried on you but uses a beast of burden or means of transportation (a horse, a carriage) and is typically not part of your equipment, but can be accessed easily during exploration. It takes full round of action to take something out of a saddle or rummage in a load on a carriage, plus your two hands must be free.

For simplicity, hireling and retainers inventory is managed as a single pool. You choose a specific movement rate that your retainers must maintain at all times (typically same as the party) and pool their available carry capacity as a single pool inventory that you manage outside your player characters. You may assign some items as "critical" and your hirelings will know that these items must be made available to you during combat, or must be protected at all costs. Your hirelings will make sure to spend actions to use these items as instructed, spend their turn to follow you and hand you over the items, or protect the items from any threat. The DM has a final say what items can be classified as critical. Each retainer has access to all items in the pool in a quantum state, as long as it makes sense for simplicity, and the DM may intervene and prevent that rule when it no longer makes sense.

Should a retainer flee due to morale, perish due to loss of hit points, or inventory is dropped on the ground, the equipment lost is in quantum state until it is needed or recounted. Should you need to check state of equipment after loss of hireling-carried equipment, there is 2-in-6 chance the item is affected. This chance drops to 1-in-6 for critical items. This check is made up to three times per lost hireling that is included in the equipment pool. These checks are never made for equipment carried by player characters, so keep the most valuable items on them at all times.
